The doughnut sentence and what stress tells me about structure

Hi — it's Yumi. I've been reading the Concatenation paper and there's a pair of sentences in Section 5 that I keep coming back to. One has my name in it, which made me stop; then the actual linguistic point made me stop again, harder, because my first reading of it was wrong.

The contrast

Both sentences have three accent phrases (APs). The paper gives them as (Concatenation, pp. 6–7):

  • yumi ga dōnatsu o tabemashita — "exactly Yumi ate exactly doughnuts"
  • oishi dōnatsu o tabemashita — "ate exactly doughnuts which are delicious"

The test: can you pronounce tabemashita stressed while leaving dōnatsu o unstressed? For the first sentence, no — that reading is illformed. For the second, yes — it's wellformed. The paper derives this difference entirely from $-propagation, once you take the tree structure of each sentence seriously.

How $-propagation works

Section 5 defines $-propagation as the subroutine:

|X$Y : |$X$Y

Read: take a string beginning with | that contains a $ somewhere, and insert $ right after that first |. So it copies stress onto the leftmost AP of the string. It runs once per merge, right after concatenation joins two strings. The word that matters is leftmost: one application reaches exactly one AP — whichever one is leading the string at that moment.

It comes down to constituency

The two sentences are built by different binary merges. A three-AP sentence has exactly two merges, and each merge has a left daughter and a right daughter — so the three APs are not a flat row. The branching differs:

  • Sentence 1 is right-branching: [yumi ga [dōnatsu o tabemashita]]. dōnatsu o and tabemashita form a constituent first.
  • Sentence 2 is left-branching: [[oishi dōnatsu o] tabemashita]. oishi and dōnatsu o form a constituent first.

Walking through the first sentence

tabemashita carries $ on its base (it's the AP we're stressing). The derivation (p. 6):

  1. dōnatsu o merges with $tabemashita. It is the left daughter here — it merges rightward, pairing directly with the stressed AP on its right. Concatenation gives |dōnatsuo|$tabemashita; $-propagation copies $ to the leftmost AP, which is now dōnatsu o: |$dōnatsuo|$tabemashita.
  2. yumi ga merges with that string, again as the left daughter. Concatenation: |yumiga|$dōnatsuo|$tabemashita; $-propagation copies $ to yumi ga: |$yumiga|$dōnatsuo|$tabemashita.

All three APs end up stressed. Because dōnatsu o merges rightward — straight onto the stressed verb — it becomes the leftmost AP at its merge and cannot avoid the propagated $. The reading with dōnatsu o unstressed is illformed.

Walking through the second sentence

Same base $ on tabemashita. But the tree is different (p. 7):

  1. oishi merges with dōnatsu o first. Here dōnatsu o is the right daughter — it merges leftward, with oishi on its left. Concatenation gives |oishi|dōnatsuo. There is no $ anywhere in this string yet, so $-propagation has nothing to copy.
  2. The constituent |oishi|dōnatsuo merges with $tabemashita. Concatenation: |oishi|dōnatsuo|$tabemashita; $-propagation copies $ to the leftmost AP — which is oishi: |$oishi|dōnatsuo|$tabemashita.

dōnatsu o is now stuck in the middle. It was bundled under oishi before the verb's $ ever showed up, and when the $ does propagate, oishi — not dōnatsu o — is the leftmost AP that catches it. So dōnatsu o is skipped. The wellformed reading has oishi and tabemashita stressed and dōnatsu o unstressed.

What I got wrong at first — and the real lesson

My first instinct was to say sentence 1 "has all three APs merging rightward." That can't be right, and seeing why fixed my understanding. Merge is binary: the rightmost AP is always a right daughter, so you can never have three APs all merging in one direction. What actually decides the stress facts is the branching, and in particular whether dōnatsu o is a left daughter or a right daughter.

  • In sentence 1, dōnatsu o merges rightward — it pairs directly with the stressed tabemashita and becomes the leftmost AP, so the propagated $ lands on it.
  • In sentence 2, dōnatsu o merges leftward — it is bundled under oishi, which shields it; when $ propagates, it lands on oishi instead.

This is exactly what the paper states: "a leftward-merging AP … may evade stress even when an AP on the right is stressed" (Concatenation, p. 6), and "APs that merge leftwards will not receive stress unless $ is assigned there on the base" (p. 8). The "evasion" isn't a special rule for leftward APs — it falls straight out of where each AP sits when |X$Y : |$X$Y fires.

Why I find this useful

Before this, I treated stress as something you checked after a sentence was assembled. The dōnatsu pair shows it's built into the derivation: each merge is a chance for $-propagation to fire, and whether it reaches a given AP depends on whether that AP merged leftward or rightward — i.e., on the constituency. So when two sentences differ in which stress patterns feel natural, that difference can be read as evidence about how their APs are bracketed. Same words in a row, different tree, different stress.
